# Li & Fung DCF Interview Pack

用途：准备 DBS 物流 / 供应链企业潜在香港资本市场项目的经历深挖。本文是内部复习材料；正式面试默认将客户描述为 `a potential Hong Kong IPO client in the supply-chain sector`，不主动披露客户名称或内部估值区间。

## 先统一口径

- **项目目的**：为潜在香港资本市场交易建立 equity story、DCF 和交易可比公司估值，并支持 senior bankers 判断定位、估值和投资者接受度。
- **个人贡献**：分析历史财务，建立五年经营预测和 UFCF，计算 WACC 和 terminal value，做敏感性及 comps cross-check，并把估值和 equity story 连起来。
- **不要夸大**：使用 `supported / built / reviewed / analysed / prepared`；不要说自己决定了最终估值、主导客户会议或独立赢得 pitch。
- **商业结论**：低增长、轻资产的供应链服务 turnaround，全球供应商网络和 supply-chain intelligence 提供上行空间；收入恢复和利润率兑现是核心风险。
- **估值结论**：工作模型的 enterprise value 约为 US$1.55bn。完成 net cash 和非普通股权益调整后，普通股价值约为 US$1.1bn–US$1.25bn。面试中只在被明确追问且不违反保密要求时使用。

## 1. Walk me through the project.

**Interview-ready answer**

At DBS Asia Capital, I supported an origination pitch for a potential Hong Kong capital-markets client in the supply-chain sector. The company helps major retailers and consumer brands design, source, produce and deliver apparel and general merchandise through a network spanning roughly 40 markets. The central question was how to present a mature, low-margin sourcing business as a credible public-market investment while remaining disciplined on valuation.

My work had three connected parts. First, I analysed the historical financials and translated the business story into measurable drivers, particularly customer conversion, sourcing diversification, margin recovery and working-capital efficiency. Second, I built and reviewed a five-year unlevered DCF, using revenue growth, EBIT margin, capital expenditure and working capital to forecast free cash flow. I calculated WACC, terminal value and valuation sensitivities, then cross-checked the output against trading comparables and a 7.0 times exit EV-to-EBITDA multiple. Third, I helped connect the valuation to the equity story and investor positioning. My role was analytical support; senior bankers retained ownership of the final client recommendation and valuation range.

## 2. What does the company do, and how does it make money?

**Interview-ready answer**

The company is a global supply-chain manager for retailers and consumer brands. It does not mainly manufacture products in its own factories. Instead, it coordinates product design, sourcing, supplier selection, production, quality control, compliance and delivery across a broad third-party supplier network. That makes the model relatively asset-light but operationally complex, because value comes from relationships, data, execution and the ability to manage orders across many markets.

It earns revenue through two broad models. Under principal trading, it purchases goods from suppliers and resells them to customers, so the full product value appears as revenue and the procurement cost appears in cost of goods sold. Under an agency or service model, it manages part of the supply chain and earns a commission or service fee. The mix matters because principal trading creates higher reported revenue but lower percentage margins, whereas agency revenue is smaller but more margin-accretive. In 2025, the company generated approximately US$6.05 billion of revenue and a gross margin of roughly 7%. The investment case therefore depended less on rapid market growth and more on customer mix, execution efficiency and modest margin recovery.

## 3. What exactly did you do on the project?

**Interview-ready answer**

I supported the valuation workstream from historical analysis through to presentation-ready outputs. I first organised the income statement, balance-sheet and cash-flow information and analysed the main movements in revenue, gross profit, operating expenses, working capital, cash and debt. That gave me a clean base from which to separate recurring operating performance from items that should not drive the forecast.

I then built the operating assumptions for a five-year DCF. For revenue, I assessed customer conversion, sourcing diversification and customer mix. For profitability, I reviewed gross margin, personnel costs, merchandising expenses and the potential efficiency benefit from more standardised and technology-enabled processes. I used these assumptions to forecast EBIT and derive unlevered free cash flow after tax, D&A, capital expenditure and working-capital investment. I also calculated the WACC, terminal value and two-dimensional sensitivities, and reviewed a peer set covering sourcing and logistics businesses. Finally, I summarised the key valuation drivers and risks for the pitchbook. I did not determine the final client valuation or lead meetings; my responsibility was to provide reliable analysis for the senior team.

## 4. Why did you use a DCF as the primary methodology?

**Interview-ready answer**

I used a DCF as the primary methodology because the company did not have a single clean listed comparable and its value depended heavily on its own cash-flow recovery. It sits between sourcing services, principal trading, consumer-product manufacturing support and supply-chain technology, so each possible peer group has different economics. Apparel manufacturers own more production assets, global logistics companies have different infrastructure and margin profiles, and software companies would overstate the value of the company’s still-emerging digital capabilities.

A DCF allowed me to value the business using assumptions directly tied to its operating model: low-single-digit revenue growth, gradual EBIT-margin recovery, limited capital intensity and relatively efficient working capital. It also made the debate transparent. If an investor disagreed with the pace of margin improvement or the long-term growth rate, we could show the valuation impact explicitly rather than hide the difference inside a selected multiple. I still used trading comparables and an exit-multiple approach as cross-checks because a DCF can create false precision. The conclusion came from triangulating intrinsic cash-flow value with how public markets price adjacent business models, rather than mechanically averaging the methods.

## 5. Walk me through your DCF.

**Interview-ready answer**

I built a five-year unlevered DCF beginning with 2025 revenue of approximately US$6.05 billion. In the base case, I forecast revenue growth of 3%, 4%, 3.5%, 3% and 2.5% from 2026 to 2030, which produced 2030 revenue of roughly US$7.08 billion. I then forecast EBIT margin increasing from approximately 1.4% in 2025 to 1.8% in 2026 and 2.5% by 2030 as customer mix and operating efficiency improved.

For each year, I calculated unlevered free cash flow as EBIT multiplied by one minus the 20% tax rate, plus D&A, minus capital expenditure and the increase in net working capital. UFCF rose from about US$80 million in 2026 to US$131 million in 2030. I discounted those cash flows at a WACC of approximately 9.8% using a mid-year convention. I calculated terminal value using a 2% perpetual-growth rate and cross-checked it with a 7.0 times exit EV-to-EBITDA multiple. The DCF produced enterprise value, after which I completed the bridge for net cash and non-common claims to derive common equity value.

**Word count: 172**

## 6. Why did you use unlevered free cash flow and WACC rather than FCFE?

**Interview-ready answer**

I used unlevered free cash flow because the purpose was to value the operating business independently of its current financing mix. UFCF represents cash available to both debt and equity providers, so it excludes interest expense and net borrowing. I calculated it as EBIT after tax, plus D&A, less capital expenditure and the increase in operating net working capital. Because the cash flow belongs to all capital providers, the matching discount rate is WACC, and the result is enterprise value.

That approach was appropriate for an IPO-oriented valuation because the company’s capital structure could change around a transaction. Using FCFE would require me to forecast interest expense, debt repayments and new borrowing explicitly, and the output would be more sensitive to financing decisions rather than operating performance. With UFCF, I could compare the company more consistently with peers and then perform a separate enterprise-to-equity bridge. I added cash and other non-operating assets and deducted debt and other non-common claims to derive value attributable to ordinary shareholders. The key principle is consistency: UFCF must be discounted at WACC, while FCFE would be discounted at the cost of equity and would produce equity value directly.

## 7. What were your key revenue assumptions and drivers?

**Interview-ready answer**

My base case assumed a recovery to low-single-digit growth rather than a sharp rebound. I forecast annual growth of 3%, 4%, 3.5%, 3% and 2.5% from 2026 to 2030, increasing revenue from approximately US$6.05 billion to US$7.08 billion. The declining growth profile also ensured that the business approached a more mature state before the terminal period.

I focused on three drivers. First was new-customer conversion: recently won accounts could support growth, but I assumed orders would ramp gradually rather than contribute at full scale immediately. Second was sourcing diversification. Tariffs and geopolitical uncertainty encourage retailers to diversify production beyond China, increasing the value of a supplier network across roughly 40 markets. Third was customer mix. The company was shifting away from slower-growing, higher-credit-risk department stores and towards discounters, big-box and club retailers, which could improve both volume resilience and revenue quality. I did not model a separate stream of AI or software revenue because there was insufficient evidence on pricing or paid adoption. Instead, I reflected digital tools through better retention, order conversion and operating efficiency.

**Word count: 178**

## 8. What were your key margin assumptions and drivers?

**Interview-ready answer**

The base case assumed gradual rather than immediate margin recovery. EBIT margin was approximately 1.4% in 2025, and I forecast it rising to 1.8% in 2026, then 2.0%, 2.2%, 2.4% and 2.5% by 2030. That progression roughly doubled EBIT from about US$87 million to US$177 million, so I treated margin delivery as the most important operating assumption in the valuation.

There were three drivers. First, customer and service mix could modestly improve gross margin if the company increased its exposure to better-quality principal-trading accounts and higher-value agency services. Second, operating leverage could reduce personnel, merchandising and administrative costs as a percentage of revenue once the top line returned to growth. Third, supply-chain intelligence tools could shorten trend research, design and sampling cycles, improving employee productivity and customer conversion. I did not assume that these tools created a high-margin software business, because paid digital revenue had not been demonstrated. The downside case therefore held margins closer to historical levels, while approximately 3% EBIT margin was reserved for a stronger execution case. This framing kept the base case dependent on measurable efficiency rather than an unproven technology premium.

**Word count: 188**

## 9. How did you forecast D&A and capital expenditure?

**Interview-ready answer**

I treated the company as an asset-light supply-chain manager rather than a manufacturer or warehouse owner, so D&A and capital expenditure were not the main valuation drivers. In the base case, D&A was approximately 0.6% of revenue, consistent with the recent combined depreciation and amortisation profile. Capital expenditure was approximately 0.72% of revenue, or 120% of D&A, which produced modest positive net investment throughout the forecast.

The reason for keeping capital expenditure above D&A was to avoid creating free cash flow through underinvestment. Even an asset-light company needs spending on technology, systems, offices and other operating infrastructure, particularly if its equity story includes digital supply-chain capabilities. At the same time, I did not apply the much higher capital intensity of manufacturers or logistics infrastructure companies, because that would not match the business model. I would ideally support the percentages with a fixed-asset and intangible-asset schedule separating maintenance from growth investment. For the pitch-stage model, however, a revenue-based assumption was proportionate to the available disclosure. I also tested higher capital expenditure in the upside growth case so that faster growth did not appear costless.

**Word count: 187**

## 10. How did you think about working capital and the negative cash-conversion cycle?

**Interview-ready answer**

The company historically operated with negative working capital because supplier payment terms could exceed the combined inventory and receivables cycle. In 2025, days sales outstanding were about 33 days, days inventory about 8 days and days payable about 48 days, producing a negative cash-conversion cycle. Economically, that means suppliers partly fund the operating cycle, and growth can potentially release rather than consume cash if those terms remain stable.

For the base-case DCF, I chose not to rely on a large working-capital benefit. I modelled a small cash use equal to approximately 1% of incremental revenue, which was a conservative way to allow for customer mix changes, timing volatility and the risk that supplier terms normalise. I then used DSO, DIO and DPO as an operating sanity check. The downside case assumed weaker collection and payment dynamics, while the upside case assumed the company preserved its negative cycle. In a more detailed model, I would forecast receivables, inventory and payables directly from their respective turnover days. The key point was that working capital should reflect the business model and scenario, rather than simply applying a generic positive percentage of revenue.

## 11. How did you structure the bear, base and bull cases?

**Interview-ready answer**

I changed operating assumptions across the scenarios rather than only adjusting the terminal value. In the bear case, revenue growth remained around 1% to 2%, EBIT margin reached only about 1.8% by 2030, and working capital consumed more cash. This represented weak consumer demand, slower customer wins and limited operating leverage. I also used a lower 1.5% perpetual-growth rate.

The base case assumed revenue growth of 3%, 4%, 3.5%, 3% and 2.5%, with EBIT margin improving from 1.8% in 2026 to 2.5% by 2030. D&A remained around 0.6% of revenue, capital expenditure around 0.72%, and working capital was a small cash use. The bull case assumed faster early growth of 5% to 6%, a 3% terminal EBIT margin, higher reinvestment and better working-capital efficiency, with a 2.25% perpetual-growth rate. I kept the WACC broadly constant so that the scenario differences came from business performance rather than mixing operating and market assumptions. This structure made the valuation debate concrete: what customer conversion, margin delivery and cash conversion would need to occur for each outcome to be credible.

**Word count: 177**

## 12. How did you calculate WACC?

**Interview-ready answer**

I calculated WACC as the market-value weight of equity multiplied by the cost of equity, plus the weight of debt multiplied by the after-tax cost of debt. For the cost of equity, I used CAPM. The inputs were a 4.32% US ten-year Treasury yield, a 5.5% equity risk premium and a levered beta of approximately 1.07, producing a cost of equity of about 10.2%.

The levered beta came from a 0.90 unlevered beta adjusted for the company’s debt-to-equity ratio and a 20% tax rate. For debt, the working model used a pre-tax cost of approximately 9.85%, based on interest expense divided by average gross debt. After the tax shield, that became roughly 7.88%. Applying approximately 80.8% equity and 19.2% debt weights produced a WACC of 9.76%, which I rounded to 9.8% in discussion. I then tested the result rather than treating it as exact. The company’s BB credit profile and exposure to discretionary retail supported a meaningful risk premium, so I included a broader WACC range, particularly 10% to 11%, in the sensitivity analysis.

**Word count: 174**

## 13. How did you choose beta and the cost of debt?

**Interview-ready answer**

For beta, I started with businesses exposed to sourcing, supply-chain services and adjacent logistics activities. Because the company was not publicly traded, I would not use a directly observed raw beta. Instead, I would unlever the comparable-company betas to remove their financing effects, take a median to reduce the impact of outliers, and relever that beta using the target company’s capital structure. The working model used a 0.90 unlevered beta and a 1.07 relevered beta.

For the cost of debt, the model used interest expense divided by average gross debt, producing approximately 9.85% before tax. I treated that as a practical estimate, not a perfect marginal borrowing cost, because historical interest expense can reflect older coupons and financing fees. I therefore checked whether it was directionally consistent with the company’s BB credit rating and outstanding bond economics. In a live transaction, I would prefer the current yield to maturity on traded debt or the risk-free rate plus a rating-based credit spread. After applying the 20% tax rate, the working after-tax debt cost was approximately 7.88%. The purpose of both inputs was to reflect current operating and financing risk, not to force WACC towards a desired valuation.

**Word count: 196**

## 14. How did you calculate terminal value, and why were the assumptions reasonable?

**Interview-ready answer**

I used the perpetuity-growth method as the primary terminal-value approach and the exit-multiple method as a cross-check. Under the perpetuity method, I grew 2030 unlevered free cash flow by 2% and divided it by WACC minus the perpetual-growth rate. A 2% rate was reasonable because the business should be mature by the end of the forecast, and long-term growth should not exceed the nominal growth of the economies and consumer markets it serves indefinitely.

For the cross-check, I applied a 7.0 times EV-to-EBITDA multiple to 2030 EBITDA. That multiple sat between the lower valuation of sourcing and apparel-related peers and the higher valuation of selected global logistics businesses, which was appropriate for a mixed but asset-light supply-chain model. I also reversed the perpetuity result into an implied terminal EV-to-EBITDA multiple of roughly 7.9 times. The fact that it was reasonably close to the 7.0 times cross-check gave me more comfort than either method alone. I did not view convergence as proof, so I still sensitised WACC, perpetual growth and exit multiple and focused on the valuation range rather than a single terminal assumption.

## 15. What were the most important sensitivities?

**Interview-ready answer**

The largest sensitivities were EBIT-margin delivery, WACC and the terminal assumptions. Margin mattered because the company started from a low base: EBIT margin was only about 1.4% in 2025, while the base case reached 2.5% by 2030. A relatively small change in margin therefore had a large percentage effect on EBIT, free cash flow and terminal value. This was more important commercially than arguing over a few basis points of near-term revenue growth.

WACC and perpetual growth were also significant because terminal value represented roughly 73% of enterprise value in the working model. At a 2% growth rate, moving WACC from 9.5% to 10.0% reduced the implied share value from approximately HK$1.59 to HK$1.50 before additional non-common equity adjustments. I therefore showed a two-dimensional WACC and growth table rather than only the base case. I also tested exit multiples from 5.0 to 9.0 times. My conclusion was not that one cell was correct, but that a defensible range required both operating execution and terminal assumptions to be reasonable. For a client discussion, I would lead with margin and cash conversion, then show how market discount rates affect the output.

**Word count: 191**

## 16. How did you select and use comparable companies?

**Interview-ready answer**

I avoided using one undifferentiated peer group because the company combines sourcing, principal trading and supply-chain services. I created two reference sets. The first contained sourcing and apparel supply-chain companies such as PDS, Lever Style, Crystal International, Hansae and Shenzhou. The second contained logistics and freight businesses such as KLN Logistics, DSV, Kuehne+Nagel, DHL and GXO. I compared geography, service mix, asset intensity, growth, margins and scale before interpreting the multiples.

EV-to-EBITDA was the most useful metric because it was less affected by financing and tax differences, while EV-to-sales provided a secondary check where profitability varied. I used P/E cautiously because non-operating items and capital structures reduced comparability. I also focused more on medians than averages because the set included large global businesses with premium valuations. The sourcing peer median was around 5.8 times trailing EV-to-EBITDA, while the selected logistics median was around 7.2 times. That made a 7.0 times exit cross-check defensible, but I did not apply it mechanically. The DCF remained primary, and comps showed how public investors might price the company’s blended business and execution risk.

## 17. Walk me through the enterprise-value-to-equity-value bridge.

**Interview-ready answer**

The DCF discounted unlevered free cash flow at WACC, so the initial output was enterprise value attributable to all capital providers. The base perpetuity-growth case produced enterprise value of approximately US$1.55 billion. To reach common equity value, I then added non-operating assets and deducted claims that rank ahead of ordinary shareholders.

The company had approximately US$461 million of cash and US$325 million of debt, so net cash was roughly US$136 million. That would increase equity value. However, the bridge also needed to reflect approximately US$605 million of perpetual securities and any other non-common claims, including relevant non-controlling interests or debt-like obligations. Those items cannot be ignored simply because accounting may classify some perpetual instruments as equity; economically, their distributions and priority reduce value available to ordinary shareholders. After incorporating the major adjustments and applying a more conservative operating range, I arrived at common equity value of roughly US$1.1 billion to US$1.25 billion. In a live transaction, I would reconcile every bridge item to the latest balance sheet and make sure leases, associates, pensions and minority interests were treated consistently with the EBITDA and cash-flow definitions.

## 18. What was your valuation conclusion, and what did it mean for the pitch?

**Interview-ready answer**

The working DCF produced enterprise value of approximately US$1.55 billion under the base operating assumptions, a 9.8% WACC and a 2% perpetual-growth rate. After adding net cash, deducting the perpetual securities and considering a more conservative long-term margin range, I viewed approximately US$1.1 billion to US$1.25 billion as a defensible common equity range. I would not volunteer the exact client range in an external interview, but I would explain the direction and the drivers.

The implication was that the pitch could not rely on a technology label or broad Asian growth themes to justify a premium valuation. Public investors would need evidence that new customer wins were converting into revenue, the shift in customer mix improved quality, and supply-chain intelligence translated into measurable efficiency. The company’s asset-light model and global supplier network supported value, but low margins and discretionary consumer exposure limited how much premium could be justified upfront. Therefore, the recommended positioning was a disciplined turnaround with technology-enabled upside, not a high-growth software or pure logistics story. That framing aligned the equity narrative with what the cash-flow model could actually support.

## 19. What were the main risks, and what would change your view?

**Interview-ready answer**

The first risk was demand. The company serves retailers and consumer brands, so weak discretionary spending or customer inventory reductions could delay the low-single-digit revenue recovery. The second was execution on margins. Because the starting EBIT margin was only about 1.4%, the valuation depended heavily on reaching the low-to-mid-2% range without sacrificing service quality or customer retention. Third, the value of supply-chain intelligence remained partly optional: digital tools could improve productivity, but I would not assign a software multiple without clear paid adoption or recurring revenue.

Other risks included customer concentration, credit exposure to weaker retailers, adverse sourcing or tariff developments, and deterioration in supplier payment terms. The capital structure also mattered because perpetual securities reduced the value attributable to ordinary shareholders. I would become more positive if new-account wins converted into repeat orders, EBITDA margin sustainably exceeded roughly 2.7%, cash conversion remained strong and digital capabilities produced measurable commercial benefits. I would move towards the downside case if revenue remained negative, margin gains came mainly from temporary cost cuts, receivable days increased or the company required more working capital and capital expenditure than expected. Those indicators directly connect operating evidence to the DCF.

## 20. What was the hardest judgement, and what did you learn?

**Interview-ready answer**

The hardest judgement was separating an attractive strategic narrative from assumptions that public investors would actually underwrite. The company had credible advantages: a supplier network across roughly 40 markets, long-standing customer relationships and tools that could improve trend analysis and product development. However, those advantages did not automatically justify aggressive growth, a software valuation or immediate margin expansion. I had to translate each qualitative claim into a financial mechanism and decide how much evidence supported it.

For example, I reflected supply-chain intelligence through customer retention, conversion and productivity rather than creating unsupported standalone technology revenue. I assumed gradual margin improvement and used bear, base and bull cases so the valuation showed what execution was required. I also used sourcing and logistics peers as reference points without pretending either group was perfectly comparable. The main lesson was that good valuation work is not primarily about making the spreadsheet more complex. It is about maintaining a clear chain from business evidence to assumptions, cash flow, valuation and investor messaging. My contribution was helping the senior team make that chain explicit and presentation-ready, while being precise about uncertainty and my own level of responsibility.
